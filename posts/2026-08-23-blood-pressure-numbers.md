If you check your blood pressure at home, you've seen it: two numbers, one on top of the other, like 118/76. Most people are never told clearly what those numbers mean or when to worry. Here's a plain-language guide.

## The two numbers

Blood pressure is written as one number over another, read as "118 over 76."

- The **top number (systolic)** is the pressure in your arteries when your heart beats.
- The **bottom number (diastolic)** is the pressure when your heart rests between beats.

Both matter. If one number lands in a higher category than the other, doctors go by the higher of the two.

## What the categories mean

The American Heart Association groups blood pressure into these ranges:

- **Normal:** below 120 and below 80.
- **Elevated:** 120–129 on top and below 80 on the bottom. A signal to focus on healthy habits before it rises further.
- **High blood pressure, Stage 1:** 130–139 on top, or 80–89 on the bottom.
- **High blood pressure, Stage 2:** 140 or higher on top, or 90 or higher on the bottom.
- **Very high (crisis level):** higher than 180 on top and/or higher than 120 on the bottom.

A single high reading doesn't mean you have high blood pressure — readings move around during the day. What matters is the pattern over time, which is exactly why checking regularly at home is so useful.

## Getting an accurate reading

Small things change the number quite a bit. For a reliable reading, the AHA suggests:

- Avoid caffeine, exercise, and smoking for 30 minutes beforehand.
- Sit quietly for about 5 minutes first, back supported, feet flat on the floor.
- Rest your arm on a table so the cuff is at about heart level.
- Don't talk during the measurement.
- Take two readings a minute apart, at the same time each day, and log them.

## When to call your care team — and when to call 911

- If your readings are landing in **Stage 1 or Stage 2** over several days, that's worth a conversation with your cardiologist or care team. It doesn't necessarily mean medication — often the first step is lifestyle changes — but they should know.
- If you get a reading **higher than 180/120**, wait a minute and take it again. If it's still that high, call your care team promptly.
- If a reading that high comes **with symptoms** — chest pain, shortness of breath, back pain, numbness or weakness, vision changes, or trouble speaking — **call 911.** Don't wait.

Knowing your numbers is one of the simplest, most powerful things you can do for your heart. You don't need to memorize the whole chart — just log your readings, watch the trend, and bring it to your care team. That's a conversation that tends to go a lot better when you show up with the data.
